AI disruption of mathematics
The advent of modern AI tools is disrupting many
long-established professions.
My own field of mathematical research – which has been
largely stable for almost a century – has proven to be
particularly susceptible to this type of disruption.

=== PAGE 3 ===
The historical era of proof scarcity
Traditional mathematics (or “Math 1.0”) operated under the
assumption of proof scarcity.
Finding a solution to an open math problem (and proving it
to be correct) was difficult and rare, and often required
significant human expertise to obtain.
Many of the institutions, traditions, incentives, and stated
objectives of Math 1.0 have been organized under this
proof scarcity assumption.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 4 ===
Objective verifiability
On the other hand, proofs have the property — unusual in
most other disciplines — of being objectively verifiable.
Different mathematicians may disagree on what types of
mathematics are interesting or fruitful.
But there is broad consensus on what constitutes a correct
and complete proof of a given problem.
In fact, it is even possible to mechanically check proofs by
computer, if they are written in an appropriate proof
assistant language (e.g., Lean).
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 5 ===
Digitizability and high-quality data
Proofs also have the property being highly digitizable; they
do not require a physical instantiation, any real world data
as input, or any real world experiment to verify the output.
In principle, a proof can be generated and verified by pen
and paper, using only standard mathematical textbooks or
libraries to supply definitions.
The solution of a complex math problem often also
requires knowledge of the appropriate research literature;
but an enormous amount of this literature is now available
digitally.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 6 ===
The era of proof abundance
This combination of objective verifiability, digitizability, and
large, high-quality data sets have made the task of solving
mathematical problems particularly amenable to the
machine learning techniques that power modern AI.
Frontier AI labs have thus prioritized advances in
mathematical problem-solving capability, ahead of other
applications which lack one or more of the above three
features.
As a consequence of this massive investment, we are now
entering an era of proof abundance in mathematics.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 7 ===
The jagged capability of AI
Given sufficient compute and access to frontier AI models,
one can now obtain solutions to many open problems that
were previously thought difficult, in relatively short amounts
of time.
Increasingly often, no special expertise from the user
prompting the AI is required to obtain such solutions.
This is in stark contrast to current AI performance on
tasks which are subjective, dependent on real world
interactions, or for which data is scarce.
AI performance is thus extremely jagged: astounding in
some directions, while inadequate in others. This is true
both within mathematics, and more broadly.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 8 ===
The new frontiers of mathematics
Despite the impression given by press releases and social
media, most of the problems at the current frontier of
mathematics remain unsolved, even with massive
computational expenditure.
Indeed, the space of mathematical problems remains
infinite; and the difficulty level of such problems is
unbounded.
Because of this, the future frontiers of mathematics will
always continue to expand.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 9 ===
Aligning goals with the long-term health of math
Furthermore, problem solving, while historically one of the
most visible goals of research mathematics, is only a proxy
for more valuable long-term goals.
This proxy was largely accurate in the era of proof scarcity,
but has become misaligned in the era of proof abundance.
Further blind optimization of problem-solving alone is now
actively harmful to the long-term health of mathematics.
The future of mathematics — “Math 2.0” — will require both
expanding the research frontier, while simultaneously
decentering the traditional role of problem solving.
Terence Tao

=== PAGE 13 ===
A new era of experimental mathematics
We now have the capability to collect large-scale
mathematical data sets that were impossible or hugely
impractical to generate in the past.
Early examples include the Equational Theories Project
and the SAIR/LMFDB Inverse Galois Problem challenge.
With such data sets, one can perform new types of
experimental mathematics: “population surveys” rather
than “case studies”.
This will help map out the new “difficulty landscape” of the
research frontier, which has a significantly different shape
now that powerful AI tools are available.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

Equational Theories Project (2024–2025)
The ETP crowdsourced both human and automated arguments

=== PAGE 15 ===
Inverse Galois Problem challenge (2026)
The first stage of IGP24 was a successful crowdsourced
competition to locate degree 24 polynomials with each of the
25, 000 possible Galois groups at this degree. The second
stage will be a collaborative effort to extend up to degree 31.

=== PAGE 16 ===
Beyond problem-solving
Contrary to popular opinion (or some of our own
marketing), mathematicians are not singularly focused on
solving open problems.
Particularly in pure mathematics, open problems serve as
“lighthouses”: not destinations to be reached in and of
themselves, but as useful guides to explore the
mathematical landscape around these problems.
The lessons learned while attempting to solve these
problems — whether they succeed, fail, or achieve partial
progress — are often more valuable than the final solution
to the problem itself.
Reaching these lighthouses prematurely by automated
tools can disrupt the exploration of the paths not taken,
and sterilize the surrounding field.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 20 ===
A thought experiment on alignment and understanding
An advanced AI is prompted: “Find a cure for cancer that
passes a stage 3 clinical trial. Make no mistakes.”
After a large amount of compute, it produces a cocktail of
previously unknown chemicals which it claims, when mixed
and injected into a patient, will kill all their cancer cells.
While nobody truly knows how this cocktail was found, the
AI (somehow) provides a Lean certificate for its prediction,
and the cocktail does indeed manage to pass a stage 3
trial.
Could the AI solution be somehow misaligned by exploiting
a weakness in the trial process or its math models?
Terence Tao

=== PAGE 25 ===
Examples of “Math 2.0” usage of AI
“Open exposition problems” as opposed to open research
problems: finding motivated explanations of results whose
known proofs are currently opaque. (The newly revised
Caltech Mathathon is an interesting experiment in this
direction.)
Collating many research papers into a larger living
network. (The Integrated Explicit Analytic Number Theory
Network is an initial attempt at such a network.)
Creation of large, high-quality libraries of formal
mathematics, such as Mathlib.
Using AI to find and highlight new principles, methods, or
insights, rather than merely new proofs.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 27 ===
The integrated explicit analytic number theory network (IEANTN)
The IEANTN is a living “spreadsheet” combining a large
number of interconnected results in explicit analytic number
theory, that can be updated in real time by adding results to, or
removing from, the network.
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

=== PAGE 28 ===
More examples
“Ablation studies”: taking an already proved theorem and
seeing whether it can still be proved after removing some
key theories or inputs (e.g., finding an elementary proof for
a result currently only provable by non-elementary means).
Automatically exploring the “neighborhood of a theorem”:
seeing what conclusions one can still salvage when one
modifies or drops hypotheses.
Developing tailored, open-source math models to
efficiently perform mathematical tasks with a high degree
of interpretability and safety. (Some open math models to
be released very soon - stay tuned!)
Terence Tao
Math 2.0

Even more examples
Creating interactive, well-organized mathematical texts or

Using expertise and insight gained from understanding

etc., etc..
The primary bottleneck to achieving “Math 2.0” is not
technology, or even institutional culture. It is imagination.
